# What to Ask a Human Office Instead

Some tasks AI should not touch at all — not because AI is useless, but
because the right person gives a better, safer answer. This chapter is your
conversion guide: for each situation, what to ask the human, and how.

## The "translate harder" reflex

When a letter is confusing, the reflex is "translate harder." Resist it.
Many confusions are not translation problems. They are *system* problems:
vague policy, missing pages, a form that exists in two versions. Translating
a confusing letter very precisely still leaves you with precisely translated
confusion. The human office can remove the confusion itself.

## Who to ask, and for what

| Situation | Ask the human | Why not AI |
|---|---|---|
| Deadline you may miss | The office on the letter (phone) | Extensions and penalties are negotiable with people, not with software |
| Confusing bill amount | Utility's or clinic's billing line | Only staff can see your actual account |
| Medical wording you must act on | Doctor, nurse line, pharmacist | Chapter 3's hard rule |
| Lease clause before signing | Landlord in writing; tenant-rights clinic for disputes | Contract meaning is a legal question |
| Government form "what do I write here" | The office's info line or in-person desk | Wrong answers here are expensive |
| Anything mentioning court, eviction, deportation, debt collection | A lawyer, legal aid, or accredited representative | Legal territory — never AI |

## The call script that works in any language

Before calling, write your question in one sentence and run it once through
translation (de-identified) so you can say it in the office's language:

> Hello, I received a letter about [topic, e.g., "a rent increase"]. I use
> a translation tool to read my mail and I want to confirm I understood
> correctly. The letter says [your understanding, e.g., "my rent changes
> in March"]. Is that correct, and what should I do before [date]?

Then ask the three grounding questions:

1. "What is the deadline, and what happens if I miss it?"
2. "What information or documents do you need from me?"
3. "Can you send that to me in writing?" *(or)* "May I record or write down
   what you said?"

Write down the date, the time of the call, and the agent's first name.
That note is your record if anything is disputed later. Keep it in file
with the letter (Chapter 7's habit).

## Requesting an interpreter is your right in many places

In many public offices — and in US healthcare settings under federal law
(the Civil Rights Act's language-access rules and related regulations) —
you can request a *qualified interpreter* at no cost. Say or write:

> I need an interpreter for [language]. Please arrange one for my
> appointment / for this call.

Do not accept "your child can interpret" for matters of health, law, or
money when a professional interpreter is available; family interpreting is
loving and helpful for small things and risky for large ones.

## When the office is the translation

Some letters exist only to trigger a human path. An invitation to "reply
before" a date, a "call us" card, a text with a shortened link — the
translation is not the point; picking up the phone is. AI's role there is
brief: identify the document's type, pull the phone number and deadline,
translate one sentence, and get out of the way.

 Certified translation can cost money; community organizations, libraries, and legal-aid clinics
often provide free or low-cost human translation and interpretation help — ask.
