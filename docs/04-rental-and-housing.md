# Rental and Housing Paperwork

Housing documents — leases, renewal offers, rent-increase notices, repair
letters, eviction warnings — are the most consequential everyday paper you
will translate. A missed clause can cost money or, in the worst case, your
home. The rules from earlier chapters apply with extra force here.

## The stakes ladder

Sort housing mail into three urgency tiers the moment it arrives:

1. **Routine:** receipts, newsletters from the property manager, holiday
   notice, community rules reminders. Translate at leisure.
2. **Money:** rent-increase notices, late-fee letters, utility cost-sharing
   statements. Translate within a day; note the amounts and dates by hand.
3. **Urgent/legal leaning:** repair or entry notices with dates, lease
   non-renewal notices, and anything from a housing authority, sheriff's
   office, or court. Same-day action: get the deadline and the phone number,
   then call a human. (Why call instead of fully relying on a translation?
   Chapter 9 explains — short version: deadlines that lead to court deserve
   a human conversation, not just a machine's guess.)

## Reading a lease in slices

Leases are long; do not translate the whole thing top-to-bottom. Ask the AI
in slices, and reassemble in a written summary:

1. **Money slice:** "Find and translate every clause about rent, deposits,
   fees, and utility charges."
2. **Dates slice:** "Find and translate every clause about lease length,
   renewal, move-out notice, and inspection or entry notice periods."
3. **Rules slice:** "Find and translate every clause about guests, pets,
   smoking, parking, and decoration."
4. **Termination slice:** "Find and translate every clause about ending the
   lease early, breaking the lease, and eviction." *(If the lease is
   non-renewed or eviction is mentioned, this is Tier 3 — a human office or
   a tenant-rights organization should hear about it from you directly.)*

Then ask for the summary you actually keep:

> Combine your answers into a one-page plain-language summary of this
> lease in [language], organized as: Money, Dates, Rules, Ending. List each
> clause as "I pay / I must / I can / They must."

The "I pay / I must / I can / They must" framing turns legalese into four
useful lists. Keep it next to the lease.

## Never sign on a translation alone

This bears repeating from Chapter 9's preview: **do not sign a lease — or
any contract — on the strength of a machine translation.** Get a
professional human translation (or a bilingual professional you trust) for
anything you will put your signature on. AI got you 90% of the way: you now
know which clauses to check and which questions to ask the landlord. That is
the honest division of labor.

## Security deposits and move-out

Deposit disputes are a classic translation trap: "normal wear and tear"
versus "damage" is a legal distinction that varies by place and machine
translators mangle it. When an itemized deduction letter arrives, translate
it, then check the deduction reasons against your move-in photos. Dispute
letters are a can-do-it-with-AI task — but send them at least a day after
drafting, and re-read Chapter 7's verification steps before you do.
