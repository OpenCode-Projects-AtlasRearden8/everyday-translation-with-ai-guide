# Medical Paperwork: Understand, Never Decide

Medical mail is where translation anxiety peaks — and where this manual is
firmest. The rule is simple and appears throughout this guide:

**AI helps you understand medical paperwork. AI never decides anything
medical, and never substitutes for a doctor, nurse, or pharmacist —
especially for anything you would sign or act on.**

That rule is not caution-mongering. A mistranslated drug name, dosage, or
date of a procedure can harm you in a way no school notice ever could. Treat
this chapter as "understanding tool, decision off-limits."

## The three jobs AI may do with medical mail

1. **Identify the document.** "Is this a bill, an appointment reminder, a lab
   result letter, or something about a claim?" Knowing the type cuts panic
   in half immediately.
2. **Translate the non-clinical parts fully.** Appointment dates, clinic
   addresses, parking instructions, billing department contacts,
   insurance-plan explanations. These are plain "paperwork language" and AI
   handles them well.
3. **Translate clinical terms into *plain language of your language*, with
   the original term kept next to them.** Example output to aim for:

   > Hypertension (original text: *hipertensión*) — means blood pressure is
   > higher than the target range. The letter does not say what to take.

   Keeping the original term alongside the translation lets a professional
   (doctor, nurse, pharmacist, certified interpreter) immediately spot and
   correct any error. That habit is a cornerstone of the verification pass
   in Chapter 7.

## Two jobs AI may never do

1. **Interpret a lab result, symptom, or diagnosis for you.** It is fine to
   ask "what does this sentence say in my language?" It is not fine to ask
   "should I be worried?" or "what should I take?" — those questions belong
   to your care team. If an AI volunteers medical advice anyway, ignore it.
2. **Stand in for a certified medical interpreter at appointments, consent
   conversations, or anything you sign.** In many places — and in most US
   healthcare settings under federal law — you have a right to a qualified
   medical interpreter at no cost. Use that right. AI in your pocket is a
   supplement between appointments, not a replacement during them. (Your
   rights and exact phrase to request an interpreter are covered in
   Chapter 9.)

## A safe workflow for medical mail

1. **Strip identifiers first.** Remove name, date of birth, member/patient
   ID, doctor's name, and any account number before pasting into a cloud
   tool (Chapter 6).
2. **Run an identification prompt:**

   > This is medical paperwork. Do not give me any medical advice. Tell me
   > only what kind of document this is (bill, reminder, result letter,
   > insurance explanation, or something else), whether there is a date by
   > which I must reply or an appointment I must attend, and what contact
   > number appears in it.
3. **Translate with anchors.** Ask that clinical terms keep the original
   word next to the translation (as in the example above).
4. **Bookend with humans.** If the letter raises any question about your
   health, write the question down — in your strongest language — and ask
   your doctor's office, nurse line, or pharmacist. Bring the translation
   with you; it will make the conversation faster and more accurate.

## What to do when the letter is unclear even after translation

That is normal and it is a signal, not a failure. Medical billing language
confuses native speakers too. The play is always the same: a short list of
written questions plus the billing or clinic phone number from the letter.
Chapter 9 gives you a template for exactly that call.
